ISM Code Yacht Requirements: What Sections 10 and 11 Actually Ask For

The ISM Code does not ask a yacht to keep more paperwork. It asks for records that can be trusted — produced on demand, attributable to a role, and intact enough to stand as evidence long after the crew who created them have rotated off. That distinction is where most vessels quietly fall short.

What the ISM Code requires — and which yachts it covers

The International Safety Management (ISM) Code is the IMO framework that obliges a company to run a documented Safety Management System (SMS): a set of procedures covering safe operation, maintenance, and emergency response, audited by the flag administration. The company holds a Document of Compliance; each vessel holds a Safety Management Certificate earned through audit [1].

For yachts, scope follows tonnage and use. Commercial yachts of 500 gross tonnes and above generally fall within ISM through their flag state and the Red Ensign Group Yacht Code, which applies the Code to large commercial yachts [2]. Private yachts are often outside the mandatory scope, yet many run an equivalent SMS anyway — because the obligations the Code creates around records are exactly the ones an owner, an underwriter, and a buyer's surveyor will later test.

Section 10: maintenance of the ship and equipment

Section 10 is the part every engineer meets first. It requires the company to establish procedures ensuring the vessel is maintained in line with applicable rules, that inspections are held at appropriate intervals, that any non-conformity is reported with its corrective action, and that records of all of this are kept [3].

It reaches further than a maintenance schedule. Section 10.3 asks the company to identify equipment and technical systems whose sudden operational failure could create a hazardous situation — critical equipment — and to put specific measures in place to promote its reliability, including testing standby arrangements that are not in continuous use [3].

Read plainly, Section 10 is a record-keeping mandate. But it is a documentation minimum, not a knowledge standard: it requires that the records exist. It does not require that they be searchable, connected to the equipment they describe, or legible to the next engineer who inherits the boat. The same records are also what a manufacturer asks for when a warranty claim turns on documented, auditable maintenance — so the gap costs money long after the audit is over.

Section 11: documentation

Section 11 governs the documents themselves. The company must control all documents and data relevant to the SMS: valid versions available at every location where they matter, reviewed and approved by authorised personnel before issue, and obsolete documents removed promptly so nobody works from a superseded procedure [1].

This is the requirement yachts most often get wrong — not because the documents are missing, but because version control is informal. On inspection day, the question is rarely "do you have a procedure?" It is "can you show this is the current one, who approved it, and when it changed?" A controlled document that cannot answer those three questions is, to an auditor, an uncontrolled one.

Records that exist but cannot be produced are, to an inspector, records that do not exist.

Where record-keeping actually fails on a yacht

The Code's intent is sound. The failure is structural, and it shows up in three places.

The first is crew rotation. A maintenance history lives partly in the planned-maintenance system and partly in the departing engineer's head and phone. When that engineer rotates off, the trail goes cold — a problem we cover in depth in our piece on crew turnover and knowledge loss.

The second is fragmentation. Faults sit in one tool, work orders in another, certificates in a folder, supplier correspondence in an inbox. The SMS assumes a single coherent record; the vessel keeps several partial ones that never quite reconcile.

The third is the inspection itself. Flag-state and Port State Control officers do not grade a binder's thickness — they look for evidence that the SMS functions: that maintenance was done, recorded, and is retrievable. Documentation-related deficiencies are a recurring cause of findings and, at the serious end, detentions [4]. An SMS that cannot produce its own records on request reads as an SMS that is not being run.

What good looks like: records that can serve as evidence

The ISM Code stops at "keep records". The standard that actually protects a vessel goes one step further: the records have to be evidence.

A maintenance log that can be edited after the fact, with no trace of the change, is not evidence — it is a note. Records that can be retroactively modified cannot serve as evidence. What turns a log into something that holds up under audit, a warranty dispute, or a casualty investigation is three properties working together: every entry is attributed to whoever made it, every entry is timestamped, and no entry is ever silently overwritten.

When those hold, the character of an inspection changes. Audit prep becomes retrieval, not assembly — you are not reconstructing a year of work the week before a survey; you are opening records that already stand on their own. The audit trail is not a feature. It is a legal defence.

Frequently asked questions

Does the ISM Code apply to private yachts?

Mandatory ISM scope generally follows commercial use and tonnage: commercial yachts of 500 gross tonnes and above typically fall in, through their flag administration and the Red Ensign Group Yacht Code [2]. Private yachts are often outside the mandatory regime, but many operate an equivalent SMS voluntarily — partly because flag or management requirements expect it, and partly because the record obligations it creates are what owners and underwriters rely on later.

What records does the ISM Code require a yacht to keep?

At minimum: evidence that the vessel is maintained to applicable rules, that inspections happen at appropriate intervals, that non-conformities are reported and corrected, and that critical equipment is identified and its reliability tested — all under Section 10. Section 11 adds that the controlling documents themselves are current, approved, and free of obsolete versions [3]. The Code requires these records to exist; it is silent on whether they are connected or searchable.

How long must a yacht retain ISM records?

Retention is set by the vessel's SMS and its flag administration rather than a single universal figure. In practice, records are kept at least across the current certificate cycle, and maintenance history is retained long enough to demonstrate the maintenance regime over time. Because warranty and casualty questions can arrive years later, keeping the full history — not just the current cycle — is the safer posture.

Is a planned-maintenance system enough for ISM compliance?

A PMS covers the scheduling and logging Section 10 expects, and it should keep its class approvals. What it rarely provides on its own is an evidentiary trail: attribution, timestamping, and an append-only history that proves a record was not altered after the fact. ISM asks that records exist; an audit, a warranty dispute, or an inspection asks that they can be trusted. Those are not the same requirement.

Summary

  • The ISM Code (Sections 10 and 11) requires yachts in scope to maintain records of maintenance, inspections, corrective action, and controlled documents — generally commercial yachts of 500 GT and above.
  • It is a documentation minimum: it requires records to exist, not that they be connected, searchable, or transferable across a crew change.
  • Record-keeping fails in practice through crew rotation, fragmented tools, and inspections that test whether the SMS actually functions.
  • Records that can be silently edited cannot serve as evidence; attribution, timestamping, and an append-only history are what make a log hold up.
  • Tie the record to the vessel, not the crew: append-only, attributed, and independently verifiable — so audit prep becomes retrieval, not assembly.
